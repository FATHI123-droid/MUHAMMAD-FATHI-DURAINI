Federal Constitution

The constitutional framework governing fundamental liberties, rights protection and the domestic legal structure within which international human rights standards may interact with Malaysian law.

01

Research Purpose

The Federal Constitution is the central domestic legal framework for examining the protection of fundamental liberties in Malaysia.

For this research project, the Constitution is examined as the principal domestic reference point for analysing the relationship between Malaysian law and international human rights standards.

Research Focus
The key issue is not whether international human rights instruments automatically become Malaysian law, but how constitutional rights, domestic legislation, judicial interpretation and international standards interact within the Malaysian legal system.
02

Constitutional Framework

The Federal Constitution is the supreme law of Malaysia. Article 4(1) provides that the Constitution is the supreme law of the Federation and that laws inconsistent with it are, to the extent of the inconsistency, void.

This establishes the constitutional framework within which legislation, executive action and judicial interpretation operate.

Article 4 Constitutional supremacy and the validity of laws inconsistent with the Constitution.
Part II Fundamental liberties and constitutional protection of specified rights.
Article 5 Protection of life and personal liberty subject to constitutional and legal conditions.
Article 8 Equality before the law and equal protection of the law, subject to the constitutional text and recognised qualifications.
03

Fundamental Liberties

Part II of the Federal Constitution contains provisions concerning fundamental liberties.

These provisions provide the principal constitutional framework for rights-based analysis in Malaysian domestic law.

Provision Subject Research Relevance
Article 5 Life and personal liberty Central to analysis concerning detention, procedural fairness and personal liberty.
Article 6 Slavery and forced labour Relevant to protection against slavery and forced labour.
Article 7 Protection against retrospective criminal laws and repeated trials Relevant to legality and procedural safeguards.
Article 8 Equality Relevant to discrimination, equal protection and constitutional classification.
Article 9 Movement Relevant to constitutional analysis concerning movement and restrictions.
Articles 10–13 Other fundamental liberties Cover specified freedoms, religious liberty, property and related constitutional protections.
04

Selected Constitutional Rights

Article 5 · Life and Personal Liberty

Article 5 is an important constitutional provision for examining the protection of personal liberty and the legal requirements surrounding deprivation of liberty.

Article 8 · Equality

Article 8 establishes equality before the law and equal protection of the law. Malaysian courts have developed constitutional jurisprudence concerning the scope and application of this guarantee.

Article 10 · Freedom of Speech, Assembly and Association

Article 10 provides constitutional protection for specified freedoms, while also recognising that Parliament may impose restrictions within the constitutional framework.

Article 12 · Rights in Respect of Education

Article 12 contains constitutional provisions concerning rights relating to education and discrimination in educational contexts.

Article 13 · Rights to Property

Article 13 provides constitutional protection concerning deprivation of property, subject to the conditions established by the Constitution.

05

Relationship with International Human Rights Law

The Federal Constitution must be distinguished from international human rights treaties. The two operate within different legal frameworks.

Malaysia's participation in an international treaty does not, by itself, necessarily transform the treaty into directly enforceable domestic law. The domestic legal effect of treaty obligations depends on the Malaysian constitutional and legislative framework.

Key Legal Distinction
International human rights obligations and domestic constitutional rights are related but legally distinct sources of norms.

This distinction is particularly important when analysing instruments such as the Convention on the Rights of the Child, CEDAW and CRPD, as well as broader international human rights standards.

06

Constitutional Interpretation

Malaysian courts have developed jurisprudence concerning the interpretation of fundamental liberties. Judicial decisions therefore form an important part of this research project.

Cases such as Lee Kwan Woh v Public Prosecutor, Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia & Anor and Alma Nudo Atenza v Public Prosecutor are examined elsewhere in R01 as part of the case-law component.

The research does not assume that a Malaysian court has incorporated an international treaty merely because an international human rights principle resembles a constitutional right. The precise reasoning of the court, the domestic legal provision involved and the status of any international instrument must be examined separately.

07

Research Relevance

The Federal Constitution provides the domestic constitutional baseline for the wider research question:

Central Research Question
How does international human rights law interact with the Malaysian domestic legal system, particularly where constitutional rights, legislation, judicial interpretation and international obligations intersect?
Domestic Law Constitutional provisions and legislation provide the primary domestic legal framework.
Judicial Interpretation Courts interpret constitutional provisions and determine the legal effect of relevant domestic rules.
International Standards Treaties, declarations and international mechanisms provide additional normative and legal reference points.
Institutional Practice Institutions such as SUHAKAM contribute to Malaysia's human rights framework and international engagement.
