01 · Research Purpose

Why begin with
the Constitution?

Any analysis of human rights protection in Malaysia must first understand the domestic legal framework within which rights are recognised, interpreted and enforced. The Federal Constitution therefore serves as the starting point of this research project.

This dossier examines the constitutional framework before considering how international human rights instruments, Malaysian courts, SUHAKAM and United Nations mechanisms interact with it.

02 · Central Research Question

The question behind
the source.

Research Question

How does Malaysia's constitutional framework shape the domestic legal protection and interpretation of fundamental rights within the broader context of international human rights law?

03 · Constitutional Architecture

The domestic legal
foundation.

The Federal Constitution establishes the constitutional structure within which Malaysian public power operates. For this research, Part II on Fundamental Liberties is particularly significant.

01

Supremacy of the Constitution

The Constitution occupies the central position within Malaysia's domestic legal order and provides the framework against which domestic laws and governmental powers are assessed.

02

Fundamental Liberties

Part II establishes constitutional protections concerning areas including personal liberty, equality, freedom of movement, speech, assembly, association and religion.

03

Judicial Interpretation

The meaning and scope of constitutional rights are developed through constitutional interpretation by Malaysian courts.

04

Limits & Restrictions

Constitutional rights must be analysed together with the textual limitations and constitutional provisions governing their exercise.

05 · Domestic / International Interface

Where international law
enters the analysis.

The Constitution must be distinguished from international human rights treaties. They operate within different legal frameworks, even though they may concern overlapping rights and principles.

Domestic Constitution

Provides the principal constitutional framework for rights that operate within Malaysia's domestic legal system.

International Treaties

May establish international obligations for Malaysia where Malaysia has become a State Party, subject to the domestic legal framework.

Interpretive Interaction

International standards may become relevant to legal analysis without that necessarily meaning the treaty itself has been incorporated into domestic law.

06 · Selected Jurisprudence

How Malaysian courts
have approached rights.

Selected Malaysian cases are examined to understand constitutional rights jurisprudence and the relationship between constitutional interpretation and international law.

Lee Kwan Woh v Public Prosecutor [2009] 5 MLJ 301
Federal Court decision concerning constitutional protections including Article 5(1) and Article 8(1), with significant discussion of constitutional interpretation and fundamental liberties.
Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia & Anor [2010] 2 MLJ 333
Federal Court constitutional jurisprudence concerning fundamental liberties and the interpretation of constitutional rights.
Alma Nudo Atenza v Public Prosecutor [2019] 4 MLJ 1
Federal Court decision examined as part of the research into constitutional rights, proportionality and the interpretation of fundamental liberties.
07 · Preliminary Finding

What this source
allows us to establish.

Preliminary Research Finding

The Federal Constitution provides the essential domestic legal starting point for analysing human rights protection in Malaysia. International human rights law therefore needs to be examined in relation to, rather than in place of, the domestic constitutional framework.

09 · Research Integrity

The source must remain
stronger than the claim.

The Constitution is examined as a legal source, not merely as a background description of Malaysian human rights.

The research distinguishes constitutional text, judicial decisions, institutional material and the researcher's own analysis. References to international human rights law are not presented as evidence that international treaties automatically form part of Malaysian domestic law.
