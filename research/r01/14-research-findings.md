# Research Findings

## Research Project 01

### Malaysia and International Human Rights Law

---

## 01 · Purpose of the Findings

This section presents the preliminary findings developed from the primary legal materials, international human rights instruments, Malaysian case law, United Nations treaty body materials, institutional materials and academic literature collected for Research Project 01.

The purpose is not to provide a final conclusion on every aspect of Malaysia's human rights framework.

Instead, this section identifies the principal legal relationships and analytical issues emerging from the research.

The central research question is:

> **How does Malaysian domestic law interact with international human rights law?**

---

# 02 · Finding One: Malaysia Operates Within a Dualist Legal Framework

The research indicates that international treaties do not automatically become part of Malaysian domestic law merely because Malaysia has become a party to a treaty.

The domestic legal effect of an international treaty depends upon the relationship between international obligations and Malaysian constitutional and legislative arrangements.

This distinction is important because treaty participation and domestic enforceability are separate legal questions.

Malaysia may assume obligations at the international level while a separate domestic legal mechanism may be required before particular treaty provisions can operate as directly enforceable rules of Malaysian domestic law.

This distinction is particularly important when analysing Malaysia's participation in international human rights treaties.

### Analytical significance

The research therefore distinguishes between:

1. Malaysia's status as a State Party to an international treaty;
2. Malaysia's international obligations under that treaty;
3. incorporation or implementation of treaty obligations through domestic legislation; and
4. the ability of Malaysian courts to apply a treaty provision as part of domestic law.

These should not be treated as interchangeable concepts.

---

# 03 · Finding Two: Ratification and Domestic Incorporation Are Distinct

Malaysia's participation in international human rights treaties demonstrates the importance of separating international legal status from domestic legal effect.

Malaysia is a State Party to several core international human rights conventions, including:

- Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC);
- Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW); and
- Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD).

The United Nations Treaty Body Database records Malaysia's treaty participation and reporting activities.

The existence of these international obligations does not, by itself, establish that every treaty provision forms part of Malaysian domestic law.

The research therefore treats the following as separate analytical questions:

> **Has Malaysia accepted the international obligation?**

and

> **Has the obligation been given domestic legal effect?**

This distinction is fundamental to understanding Malaysia's human rights framework.

---

# 04 · Finding Three: The Federal Constitution Remains the Central Domestic Legal Framework

Part II of the Federal Constitution provides the principal constitutional framework for fundamental liberties in Malaysia.

Accordingly, the domestic legal analysis of human rights begins with the Constitution rather than assuming that an international treaty automatically supplies the applicable domestic rule.

This is also reflected institutionally in the statutory framework governing SUHAKAM.

The Human Rights Commission of Malaysia Act 1999 defines "human rights" by reference to the fundamental liberties enshrined in Part II of the Federal Constitution.

This creates an important institutional connection between constitutional rights and Malaysia's national human rights institution.

---

# 05 · Finding Four: Malaysian Courts Have Developed Significant Constitutional Rights Jurisprudence

The case law examined in this research demonstrates that Malaysian courts have developed substantial jurisprudence concerning fundamental liberties.

Three cases are particularly relevant to this research project:

### Lee Kwan Woh v Public Prosecutor

**Lee Kwan Woh v Public Prosecutor [2009] 5 MLJ 301**

The Federal Court adopted a broad approach to the interpretation of fundamental liberties under the Federal Constitution.

The decision is particularly significant for the interpretation of Articles 5 and 8 and the constitutional protection of personal liberty and equality.

The case demonstrates that Malaysian constitutional rights can be developed through domestic constitutional interpretation without treating an international treaty as automatically incorporated into Malaysian law.

---

### Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia & Anor

**Sivarasa Rasiah v Badan Peguam Malaysia & Anor [2010] 2 MLJ 333**

The case is significant for the development of proportionality analysis in Malaysian constitutional law.

The decision demonstrates that constitutional limitations on fundamental liberties may require substantive judicial examination rather than merely formal consideration of legislative authority.

---

### Alma Nudo Atenza v Public Prosecutor & Another Appeal

**Alma Nudo Atenza v Public Prosecutor & Another Appeal [2019] 3 MLRA 1**

The Federal Court further developed constitutional reasoning concerning Articles 5 and 8.

Subsequent Malaysian case law has continued to refer to the proportionality approach associated with Sivarasa Rasiah and Alma Nudo Atenza.

The continuing use of these authorities indicates that constitutional human rights analysis in Malaysia has developed substantially through domestic jurisprudence.

---

# 06 · Finding Five: International Human Rights Norms and Domestic Constitutional Interpretation Must Be Distinguished

The research reveals a more complex relationship between international human rights law and Malaysian constitutional interpretation.

The proposition that treaties are not automatically part of Malaysian domestic law does not necessarily resolve every question concerning the relevance of international human rights norms to constitutional interpretation.

Malaysian case law contains discussion concerning the possible relevance of international norms and human rights standards in interpreting constitutional rights.

However, the use of international norms as interpretive material must not be confused with direct incorporation of treaty provisions into Malaysian domestic law.

This distinction is essential.

A court considering an international human rights norm as an interpretive reference is not necessarily applying that treaty as an independently enforceable domestic legal rule.

---

# 07 · Finding Six: Bato Bagi Illustrates the Domestic Effect Issue

**Bato Bagi & Ors v Kerajaan Negeri Sarawak & Another Appeal [2011] 8 CLJ 766**

is an important authority for understanding the relationship between international norms and Malaysian domestic law.

The case involved native customary rights and, among other issues, arguments concerning international norms.

The judgment contains an important statement concerning the domestic legal status of international treaties.

The case is therefore relevant to the proposition that international treaties do not automatically form part of Malaysian domestic law unless incorporated into domestic law.

### Analytical significance

Bato Bagi should not be read as meaning that international human rights law has no relevance whatsoever to Malaysian constitutional analysis.

Rather, it demonstrates the importance of distinguishing:

**international legal obligation**

from

**domestic legal enforceability**.

This distinction is one of the central findings of Research Project 01.

---

# 08 · Finding Seven: SUHAKAM Creates an Institutional Bridge Between Domestic and International Human Rights Standards

The Human Rights Commission of Malaysia Act 1999 establishes SUHAKAM as Malaysia's national human rights institution.

The Commission's statutory functions include:

- promoting awareness and education relating to human rights;
- advising and assisting the Government in formulating legislation and procedures;
- recommending measures to the Government;
- recommending subscription to or accession to treaties and other international human rights instruments;
- conducting research;
- studying and verifying alleged human rights infringements; and
- conducting other appropriate activities within its statutory mandate.

These functions are particularly relevant to the relationship between Malaysian law and international human rights standards.

SUHAKAM's published materials also demonstrate that the Commission has undertaken compatibility studies examining Malaysian law against international human rights instruments.

This indicates that the relationship between domestic law and international human rights standards is not limited to judicial proceedings.

It also operates through institutional research, policy advice, treaty engagement and human rights monitoring.

---

# 09 · Finding Eight: Treaty-Body Engagement Provides a Separate International Review Mechanism

Malaysia's interaction with United Nations human rights treaty bodies provides another layer of analysis.

The OHCHR Treaty Body Database records Malaysia's reporting cycles and documentation relating to the implementation of human rights treaties.

These materials may include:

- State party reports;
- replies to lists of issues;
- information submitted by national human rights institutions;
- information submitted by civil society organisations;
- constructive dialogue materials; and
- concluding observations.

These documents should not automatically be treated as equivalent to Malaysian domestic legislation or judicial decisions.

Their legal and institutional character is different.

Nevertheless, they provide important evidence concerning how Malaysia's implementation of international human rights obligations is examined at the international level.

---

# 10 · Finding Nine: Malaysia's Treaty Participation Is Selective

The research confirms that Malaysia's participation in the international human rights treaty system is selective rather than comprehensive.

Malaysia is party to several core human rights conventions, including:

- CRC;
- CEDAW; and
- CRPD.

At the same time, Malaysia has not become a State Party to every major core UN human rights treaty.

This creates an important analytical distinction between:

> rights recognised in the Federal Constitution,

> obligations arising from treaties to which Malaysia is a party,

> international standards contained in treaties to which Malaysia is not a party,

and

> broader international human rights norms and declarations.

These categories should not be treated as legally identical.

---

# 11 · Finding Ten: Reservations and Declarations Require Separate Examination

Malaysia's treaty participation cannot be assessed solely by asking whether Malaysia has ratified or acceded to a particular convention.

The specific terms of participation must also be examined.

This includes:

- reservations;
- declarations;
- interpretive positions;
- reporting practice; and
- subsequent withdrawals or modifications.

For example, SUHAKAM's published treaty-status material records reservations associated with Malaysia's participation in CEDAW, CRC and CRPD.

Accordingly, future research should examine the precise scope of Malaysia's treaty commitments rather than relying only on the label "State Party."

---

# 12 · Finding Eleven: International Human Rights Law Has Multiple Points of Interaction With Malaysian Law

The research identifies at least five points of interaction:

### 1. Constitutional interpretation

International human rights norms may arise as interpretive material in discussions concerning constitutional rights.

### 2. Legislative development

International standards may inform discussions concerning the development or amendment of Malaysian legislation.

### 3. Institutional practice

Institutions such as SUHAKAM may use international human rights standards in research, recommendations and policy engagement.

### 4. International reporting

Malaysia's treaty-body reporting creates an international accountability and review process.

### 5. Academic and policy analysis

International standards provide a framework for comparing Malaysian law with broader international human rights standards.

These forms of interaction should not be collapsed into a single concept of "incorporation."
