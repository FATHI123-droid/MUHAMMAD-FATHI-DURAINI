01 · RESEARCH PURPOSE

Disability rights as a legal research question

This research examines the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities as part of the broader study of international human rights law and its interaction with Malaysian law. The focus is on the relationship between international standards, domestic legislation, institutional implementation and treaty-body monitoring.

Central Research Question

How does Malaysia's engagement with the CRPD interact with its domestic legal and institutional framework for protecting the rights and participation of persons with disabilities?

02 · CONVENTION FRAMEWORK

Core principles shaping the analysis

The CRPD adopts a rights-based framework in which persons with disabilities are treated as rights-holders and active participants in society rather than merely as recipients of welfare measures.

01

Dignity

Respect for inherent dignity forms part of the Convention's rights-based approach.

02

Equality

The Convention addresses discrimination and promotes equality for persons with disabilities.

03

Participation

Participation and inclusion are central to the Convention's approach to disability rights.

04

Accessibility

Accessibility is treated as an important condition for the effective exercise of rights.

05 · DOMESTIC IMPLEMENTATION

The domestic implementation question

A treaty-based analysis should not stop at ratification. The next stage is to identify the domestic laws, institutions and policies through which rights are recognised, implemented and monitored.

01

Legal recognition

Identify the relevant constitutional provisions, legislation and regulatory instruments concerning persons with disabilities.

02

Institutional implementation

Examine the role of government agencies, public institutions and human rights bodies in translating legal standards into practice.

03

Practical accessibility

Consider whether legal recognition is accompanied by effective access to education, employment, public services, justice and participation.

04

International monitoring

Compare domestic implementation with the documentation and monitoring process conducted under the CRPD framework.

06 · RESEARCH STATUS

Where the research currently stands

The OHCHR Treaty Body Database records Malaysia's initial State party report under the CRPD as CRPD/C/MYS/1.

The research will continue to monitor the official UN treaty-body materials concerning Malaysia, including the development of the review process and any future concluding observations.

Research status: treaty-body materials require continued monitoring as the international review process develops.
07 · ANALYTICAL QUESTIONS

Questions for further research

QUESTION 01

What is the domestic legal effect of the CRPD?

The analysis must distinguish Malaysia's international treaty obligations from the domestic enforceability of treaty provisions.

QUESTION 02

How does the CRPD interact with domestic disability legislation?

The research should examine the relationship between international standards and the Persons with Disabilities Act 2008.

QUESTION 03

How should accessibility be evaluated?

Further research can examine accessibility not only as physical access but also as a condition for effective participation and enjoyment of rights.

QUESTION 04

What does treaty-body monitoring reveal?

UN documentation can provide an external source for examining implementation, challenges and areas requiring further research.

08 · PRELIMINARY FINDING

The CRPD shifts the research question from protection to participation.

The Convention provides a framework for examining disability through human rights, equality, dignity, accessibility and participation. For Malaysia, the central research task is therefore to examine how international commitments interact with domestic legislation, institutions and practical implementation rather than treating ratification as the end of the analysis.

Research Integrity

This page is a research framework and does not present a final assessment of Malaysia's compliance with the CRPD. International obligations, domestic legislation, institutional practice, empirical evidence and the researcher's own analysis must remain clearly distinguished. Conclusions should be based on primary materials and verifiable evidence.
