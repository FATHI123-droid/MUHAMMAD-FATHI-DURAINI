# 04 · INTERNATIONAL LAW AND MALAYSIAN COURTS

A central issue for later research is how Malaysian courts approach international law and international human rights standards.

The research should distinguish between:

### International treaties binding on Malaysia

Treaties to which Malaysia is a State Party may establish international obligations for Malaysia.

However, the domestic legal effect of a treaty must be considered according to Malaysian constitutional and statutory law.

### International instruments not binding on Malaysia as treaties

Documents such as the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** may have normative or interpretive relevance.

Their international significance should not automatically be equated with domestic legislation or directly enforceable domestic rights.

### Foreign and international judicial decisions

Decisions from foreign courts and international courts may provide comparative or persuasive material.

They should not automatically be treated as binding Malaysian law.

The legal status of each source must therefore be identified before it is relied upon in the research.

# 09 · DETAILED CASE ANALYSIS

## Lee Kwan Woh v Public Prosecutor

**Citation:** [2009] 5 MLJ 301
**Court:** Federal Court of Malaysia
**Subject:** Fair trial, personal liberty and constitutional interpretation

### 1. Facts

Lee Kwan Woh was charged under the Dangerous Drugs Act 1952 in relation to the possession of cannabis.

At the close of the prosecution's case, the appellant sought to make a submission that there was no case to answer.

The trial judge did not allow the appellant to make the submission and proceeded with the trial.

The appellant was subsequently convicted and sentenced to death.

The matter was brought before the Federal Court on appeal, raising issues concerning the conduct of the criminal trial and the constitutional protection of fundamental liberties.

### 2. Legal Issue

The Federal Court considered whether the appellant's right to a fair trial had been infringed by the failure to allow him to make a submission of no case to answer at the close of the prosecution's case.

The Court also considered the constitutional meaning of **personal liberty under Article 5(1)** of the Federal Constitution and its relationship with **Article 8(1)**.

### 3. Relevant Law

The principal constitutional provisions considered include:

* **Article 5(1)** — protection of life and personal liberty
* **Article 8(1)** — equality before the law and equal protection of the law

The case also involved the criminal procedure governing the stage of the trial at which the prosecution's case is completed and the accused is entitled to respond to the prosecution's evidence.

### 4. Judgment

The Federal Court allowed the appeal.

The Court found that the appellant had not been given the opportunity to make the submission of no case to answer after the prosecution had closed its case.

The Federal Court therefore set aside the conviction and sentence and ordered the appellant's release.

### 5. Judicial Reasoning

The Federal Court treated the right to a fair trial as falling within the constitutional protection of personal liberty under Article 5(1).

The judgment adopted a substantive approach to fundamental liberties and rejected an interpretation that would reduce constitutional rights to narrow or merely technical protections.

The Court's discussion of Articles 5(1) and 8(1) is significant for understanding the development of constitutional rights jurisprudence in Malaysia.

The case therefore demonstrates how procedural fairness in criminal proceedings can be connected to the constitutional protection of personal liberty.

### 6. International Law

The case should be examined alongside international human rights standards concerning fair trial and personal liberty.

Relevant international standards may include:

* Article 10 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights
* Article 11 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights
* Article 14 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

However, these international instruments must be distinguished from the domestic legal holding of the Federal Court.

The presence of an international human rights standard does not by itself establish that the standard is directly enforceable as Malaysian domestic law.

The relationship between the judgment and international human rights law should therefore be analysed separately from the domestic constitutional holding.

### 7. Legal Significance

The case is significant for the study of Malaysian constitutional law because it illustrates the Federal Court's approach to fundamental liberties and fair trial protection.

It provides material for examining:

* Constitutional protection of personal liberty
* Fair trial guarantees
* Procedural fairness
* Equality before the law
* Constitutional interpretation
* Judicial protection of fundamental liberties

### 8. Research Relevance

The case contributes to Research Project 01 by demonstrating how a Malaysian court can interpret constitutional rights in the context of criminal procedure.

It provides a basis for examining the relationship between:

**Constitutional Guarantee → Judicial Interpretation → Procedural Protection → Human Rights Protection**

Further research should examine the judgment's treatment of international human rights standards and compare the relevant constitutional protections with applicable international standards, while maintaining a clear distinction between international obligations and domestic legal effect.
