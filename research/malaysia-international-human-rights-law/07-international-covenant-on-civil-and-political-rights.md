## SOURCE IDENTIFICATION

**Title:** International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights
**Abbreviation:** ICCPR
**Adopted:** 16 December 1966
**UN Resolution:** General Assembly Resolution 2200A (XXI)
**Entry into Force:** 23 March 1976
**Source Type:** International Human Rights Treaty
**Depositary:** Secretary-General of the United Nations

The ICCPR is a major international human rights treaty concerning civil and political rights. It was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 16 December 1966 and entered into force on 23 March 1976. ([ohchr.org](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/ProfessionalInterest/ccpr.pdf))

The OHCHR country-specific treaty database does not record a signature or ratification/accession date for Malaysia in relation to the ICCPR. Malaysia is therefore treated in this research as **not a State Party to the ICCPR**. ([tbinternet.ohchr.org](https://tbinternet.ohchr.org/_layouts/15/TreatyBodyExternal/countries.aspx?CountryCode=MYS&Lang=EN))

---

# WHY THIS SOURCE MATTERS

The ICCPR is important for this research because it provides a major international framework for civil and political rights.

It also provides a useful comparative case for examining the distinction between:

* International human rights standards
* Treaty obligations
* Malaysia's domestic constitutional protections
* Malaysia's participation in selected human rights treaties
* International treaty monitoring

Unlike the CRC, CEDAW and CRPD examined earlier in this project, Malaysia is not a State Party to the ICCPR.

This distinction is legally important.

The research should therefore avoid describing ICCPR provisions as treaty obligations binding upon Malaysia in the same manner as treaties to which Malaysia is a State Party.

# ARTICLE 2 — IMPLEMENTATION AND REMEDIES

Article 2 establishes obligations for States Parties to respect and ensure the rights recognised in the Covenant and to provide effective remedies where rights are violated.

Because Malaysia is not a State Party, Article 2 should not be presented as a treaty obligation binding on Malaysia.

Instead, it can be used as:

* Comparative international law
* International human rights standards
* A reference point for domestic legal analysis
* Material for examining Malaysia's engagement with international human rights norms

---

The research should distinguish between ICCPR standards and the specific legal rules applicable under Malaysian law.

# LEGAL SIGNIFICANCE OF NON-PARTICIPATION

Malaysia's non-participation in the ICCPR is relevant to the research question concerning the relationship between domestic law and international human rights standards.

It creates a useful analytical distinction between:

### Treaty Obligations

Obligations arising from treaties to which Malaysia is a State Party.

### International Standards

Rights and principles contained in international instruments that may be used for comparative or interpretive analysis.

### Domestic Law

Constitutional and statutory rules operating within Malaysia's legal system.

These categories should not be treated as legally identical.

# COMPARISON WITH THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION

Several ICCPR rights correspond conceptually with provisions in the Federal Constitution.

| ICCPR      | Federal Constitution | Research Area             |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Article 6  | Article 5            | Life and personal liberty |
| Article 9  | Article 5            | Liberty and detention     |
| Article 18 | Article 11           | Religion                  |
| Article 19 | Article 10           | Expression                |
| Article 21 | Article 10           | Peaceful assembly         |
| Article 22 | Article 10           | Association               |
| Article 26 | Article 8            | Equality                  |

The comparison should therefore examine:

**Text → Scope → Restrictions → Judicial Interpretation → Remedies**

# HUMAN RIGHTS COMMITTEE

The ICCPR establishes the **Human Rights Committee** as the treaty-monitoring body.

The Committee examines implementation by States Parties, including through periodic reporting.

Because Malaysia is not a State Party to the ICCPR, Malaysia does not participate in the Committee's reporting process as a State Party under the Covenant.

This should be distinguished from Malaysia's participation in other UN treaty-body processes.
