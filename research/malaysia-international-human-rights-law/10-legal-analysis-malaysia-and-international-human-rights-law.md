# Legal Analysis: Malaysia and International Human Rights Law

## 01 · PURPOSE

This file develops the preliminary legal analysis for:

**Research Project 01: Malaysia and International Human Rights Law**

### Research Question

> How does Malaysian domestic law relate to international human rights standards, and what legal and institutional considerations arise from this relationship?

The analysis examines the relationship between:

**International Human Rights Standards → International Obligations → Domestic Law → Judicial Interpretation → Institutional Practice**

---

# 02 · THE DUALIST FRAMEWORK

A central issue in Malaysian international human rights law is the relationship between international treaties and domestic law.

Malaysia generally follows a **dualist approach** to the domestic application of treaties.

Under this approach, the conclusion or ratification of an international treaty does not by itself automatically make the treaty enforceable as domestic law.

The Malaysian Judiciary has described the position as requiring treaties to be incorporated into domestic law before their provisions become domestically enforceable.

This creates an important distinction between:

* Malaysia's international obligations
* Domestic legal enforceability
* Constitutional rights
* Statutory rights
* International human rights standards

These categories should not be treated as legally identical.

---

# 03 · TREATY STATUS AND DOMESTIC EFFECT

The research identifies three separate questions when examining an international human rights treaty.

### Question 1

**Is Malaysia a State Party to the treaty?**

This determines whether Malaysia has undertaken treaty obligations at the international level.

### Question 2

**Has the treaty been incorporated into Malaysian domestic law?**

This concerns whether the treaty or relevant treaty provisions have been given domestic legal effect through Malaysian legislation.

### Question 3

**How have Malaysian courts or institutions treated the treaty?**

A treaty may be relevant to legal interpretation or institutional practice even where it does not operate as directly enforceable domestic legislation.

These questions must be answered separately.

---

# 04 · RATIFICATION IS NOT THE SAME AS DOMESTIC INCORPORATION

The distinction between international obligation and domestic enforceability is particularly important for Malaysia.

The Malaysian Judiciary has discussed the position that treaty provisions become domestically enforceable where they have been incorporated through legislation.

Accordingly, research should avoid statements such as:

> "Malaysia ratified the treaty, therefore the treaty automatically forms part of Malaysian law."

A more legally precise analysis asks:

> What international obligation exists, what domestic legislation exists, and what legal effect has Malaysian law given to the relevant standard?

This distinction is one of the central analytical foundations of this research.

# 06 · INTERNATIONAL STANDARDS AND MALAYSIAN COURTS

International human rights standards may arise in Malaysian judicial reasoning in different ways.

Possible forms include:

* Direct application of domestic legislation
* Interpretation of constitutional provisions
* Interpretation of statutes
* Reference to international norms
* Comparative jurisprudence
* Academic or persuasive authority

The existence of an international instrument does not automatically establish that its provisions are binding domestic law.

The research therefore needs to distinguish between **binding domestic law** and **interpretive or persuasive material**.

The comparison should not assume that corresponding rights have identical legal meaning.

# 08 · INTERNATIONAL LAW AS AN INTERPRETIVE ISSUE

One of the important questions is whether international human rights standards can assist Malaysian courts when interpreting domestic law.

Malaysian judicial materials demonstrate that this issue has generated different approaches.

The Malaysian Judiciary has discussed cases in which international norms were considered in constitutional or human rights reasoning, while also recording judicial statements emphasising that international treaties do not form part of Malaysian law unless incorporated.

This creates an important area for case-law analysis:

**How far can international human rights standards influence interpretation without becoming domestic law?**

This question will be examined through individual judgments rather than assumed in advance.

---

# 09 · SUHAKAM AND INTERNATIONAL HUMAN RIGHTS STANDARDS

SUHAKAM is an important institutional component of the research.

The Human Rights Commission of Malaysia Act 1999 establishes functions including:

* Promoting awareness and education concerning human rights
* Advising and assisting the Government in formulating legislation and procedures
* Recommending the Government to subscribe to or accede to international instruments relating to human rights
* Inquiring into allegations of human rights infringements
* Undertaking research
* Conducting programmes and activities concerning human rights

This makes SUHAKAM particularly relevant to the institutional dimension of the research.

The analysis will examine how a national human rights institution can contribute to the relationship between:

**International Standards → National Institutions → Domestic Policy → Human Rights Protection**

# 12 · KEY LEGAL DISTINCTIONS

The research must consistently distinguish between:

### International Treaty

A legally binding international agreement between States or other subjects of international law.

### Declaration

An international instrument that may establish important standards without itself operating as a treaty.

### International Custom

A possible source of international law arising from general practice accepted as law, subject to the requirements of international law.

### Domestic Legislation

Law enacted within Malaysia through the domestic constitutional and legislative framework.

### Constitutional Right

A right protected by the Federal Constitution.

### Institutional Standard

A standard or recommendation developed by a national or international institution that may inform policy or practice.

These categories have different legal consequences.

# 13 · PRELIMINARY FINDING

The initial source collection indicates that the relationship between Malaysian law and international human rights law cannot be reduced to a simple question of whether Malaysia "follows" international human rights standards.

The more precise legal inquiry concerns:

> **Which international obligations apply to Malaysia, how those obligations interact with domestic law, what has been incorporated through legislation, how courts interpret relevant domestic provisions, and what role institutions such as SUHAKAM play in promoting human rights standards.**

This provides a more precise foundation for the research paper.

# 15 · RESEARCH LIMITATION

This preliminary analysis does not attempt to determine whether Malaysian law is generally "compliant" or "non-compliant" with international human rights law.

Such a conclusion would require:

* Detailed treaty-by-treaty analysis
* Examination of reservations
* Relevant Malaysian legislation
* Judicial decisions
* Treaty-body materials
* Institutional practice
* Academic literature
* Current government positions

The present stage establishes the legal framework for that analysis.
