# Universal Declaration of Human Rights

### International Human Rights Instrument 04

**Research Project:** Malaysia and International Human Rights Law

---

## SOURCE IDENTIFICATION

**Title:** Universal Declaration of Human Rights
**Abbreviation:** UDHR
**Adopted:** 10 December 1948
**Adopting Body:** United Nations General Assembly
**Resolution:** 217 A (III)
**Source Type:** International Human Rights Declaration

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted and proclaimed by the United Nations General Assembly on 10 December 1948 in Paris.

Unlike the CRC, CEDAW and CRPD, the UDHR is a declaration rather than a treaty.

For this research project, it therefore serves as an important international human rights standard and reference framework rather than being treated as a treaty imposing obligations on Malaysia in the same manner as a ratified or acceded treaty.

---

# WHY THIS SOURCE MATTERS

The UDHR provides one of the foundational international frameworks for understanding modern human rights.

The Declaration sets out a broad range of civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights.

These include rights relating to:

* Equality and non-discrimination
* Life, liberty and security
* Freedom from slavery
* Freedom from torture
* Recognition before the law
* Equality before the law
* Fair hearing
* Privacy
* Freedom of movement
* Freedom of thought, conscience and religion
* Freedom of opinion and expression
* Peaceful assembly and association
* Participation in government
* Work
* Social security
* Education
* Adequate standard of living
* Cultural participation

The Declaration describes itself as a **common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations**.

# ARTICLE 1 — DIGNITY AND EQUALITY

Article 1 establishes the principle that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.

This provides a foundational reference point for examining:

* Human dignity
* Equality
* Non-discrimination
* Fundamental liberties
* Human rights protection

The research should compare this principle with the constitutional framework of Malaysia, particularly the provisions concerning fundamental liberties.

---

# ARTICLE 2 — NON-DISCRIMINATION

Article 2 establishes protection against distinctions based on characteristics including race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status.

This provision can be examined alongside:

* Federal Constitution Article 8
* CEDAW
* CRPD
* CRC
* Malaysian legislation
* Domestic judicial interpretation

This provides an opportunity to examine how the broader international human rights framework relates to specific treaty regimes and Malaysian domestic law.

---

# ARTICLE 3 — LIFE, LIBERTY AND SECURITY

Article 3 recognises the rights to life, liberty and security of person.

This creates a useful comparison with:

* Federal Constitution Article 5
* International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights
* Malaysian criminal and constitutional law
* International human rights standards concerning detention and personal liberty

The research should distinguish between the wording of the UDHR, treaty obligations and domestic constitutional provisions.

---

# ARTICLE 5 — FREEDOM FROM TORTURE

Article 5 establishes protection against torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment.

This provision is particularly relevant to research concerning:

* Detention
* Law enforcement
* Places of detention
* Prison conditions
* Treatment of detainees
* International human rights standards

It can later be examined together with relevant UN materials and Malaysian institutional reports.

---

# ARTICLE 7 — EQUALITY BEFORE THE LAW

Article 7 establishes equality before the law and equal protection of the law.

For this research, Article 7 can be compared with:

**UDHR Article 7**

↓

**Federal Constitution Article 8**

↓

**Domestic legislation**

↓

**Judicial interpretation**

This provides a direct pathway for examining the relationship between international human rights standards and Malaysian constitutional law.

---

# ARTICLE 8 — EFFECTIVE REMEDY

Article 8 recognises the right to an effective remedy before competent national tribunals for acts violating fundamental rights granted by constitution or law.

This provision is particularly relevant to institutional and legal research concerning:

* Courts
* Administrative remedies
* Complaints mechanisms
* Human rights institutions
* Access to justice

It can also provide a conceptual connection to the study of SUHAKAM's complaint and inquiry functions under Act 597.

---

# ARTICLE 18 — FREEDOM OF THOUGHT, CONSCIENCE AND RELIGION

Article 18 protects freedom of thought, conscience and religion.

This provision is relevant to examining the relationship between:

* International human rights standards
* Federal Constitution Article 11
* Domestic legislation
* Judicial interpretation
* Religious freedom

The research should carefully distinguish the international standard from the specific constitutional and legal framework applicable in Malaysia.

---

# ARTICLE 19 — FREEDOM OF OPINION AND EXPRESSION

Article 19 recognises freedom of opinion and expression, including the freedom to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through different media.

This can be compared with:

* Federal Constitution Article 10
* Domestic legislation
* Judicial decisions
* International human rights standards
* UN human rights materials

The analysis should separately identify rights, permissible restrictions and judicial interpretation.

---

# ARTICLE 20 — ASSEMBLY AND ASSOCIATION

Article 20 recognises freedom of peaceful assembly and association.

This creates another direct comparison with Federal Constitution Article 10.

Potential research questions include:

* How is peaceful assembly protected under Malaysian law?
* What legal restrictions apply?
* How have Malaysian courts interpreted these restrictions?
* How does the domestic framework compare with international standards?

---

# ARTICLE 21 — PARTICIPATION IN GOVERNMENT

Article 21 concerns participation in government and equal access to public service.

This provides a broader human rights framework for examining:

* Political participation
* Public service
* Equal access
* Civic participation
* Representation

The research should distinguish the text of Article 21 from later treaty provisions and contemporary political analysis.

# ARTICLES 22–27 — ECONOMIC, SOCIAL AND CULTURAL RIGHTS

The UDHR also contains a substantial framework of economic, social and cultural rights.

Relevant provisions include:

Rest and leisure.

### Article 25

These provisions are particularly relevant to the **Human Rights & Development** component of this research project.

# ARTICLE 28 — INTERNATIONAL ORDER

Article 28 provides that everyone is entitled to a social and international order in which the rights and freedoms set forth in the Declaration can be fully realised.

This provision provides a useful bridge between:

**Human Rights**

and

**International Relations**

It can therefore support later research concerning:

* International cooperation
* International institutions
* Global governance
* International legal order
* Human rights and development

# UDHR AND MALAYSIAN LAW

The UDHR should not be treated as if it were automatically incorporated into Malaysian domestic law.

Instead, the research should examine the relationship between:

**UDHR**

↓

**International Human Rights Standards**

↓

**Treaty Regimes**

↓

**Malaysian Constitutional Law**

↓

**Malaysian Legislation**

↓

**Judicial Interpretation**

↓

**Institutional Practice**

This approach avoids conflating international declarations, treaties and domestic legal rules.

---

# UDHR AND THE TREATY SYSTEM

The UDHR should also be understood in relation to later international human rights treaties.

For this research, the following relationship is important:

**UDHR**

→ Foundational international declaration

↓

**ICCPR**

→ Civil and political rights treaty

↓

**ICESCR**

→ Economic, social and cultural rights treaty

↓

**Specialised Treaties**

→ CRC · CEDAW · CRPD

These instruments should be studied according to their individual legal status and Malaysia's participation in each instrument.

# RESEARCH QUESTIONS

1. What role does the UDHR play in the development of international human rights standards?
2. How do selected UDHR rights correspond with fundamental liberties under the Malaysian Constitution?
3. How do later human rights treaties develop rights reflected in the UDHR?
4. How should the UDHR be distinguished from binding treaty obligations?
5. How have Malaysian courts or institutions referred to international human rights standards?
6. What role does the UDHR play in understanding Malaysia's broader human rights framework?
7. How can the UDHR be used as a comparative framework without treating it as domestic legislation?

* The text of the Declaration
* UN General Assembly resolutions
* International treaties
